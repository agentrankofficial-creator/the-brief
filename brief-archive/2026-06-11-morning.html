Editor's Read
Suhail Seth · June 11, 2026

The market spent yesterday burying the Fed rate-cut trade after a 4.2% CPI print. This morning the European Central Bank did something more consequential: it actually hiked — 25 basis points, its first increase since 2023 — straight into a shooting war. That is the story.

For two months I've argued the Strait of Hormuz had stopped being an oil story and become a monetary one. The ECB just made it official. When a central bank tightens into a supply shock it cannot control, it is telling you the inflation it fears is no longer transitory — it is structural, and it is arriving through the energy line. Connect the dots: the ECB hikes, Fed futures now price a hike by year-end (a cut was consensus six weeks ago), the Bank of Canada held yesterday but quietly stripped the dovish language out of its statement, and the rupee sits at 95.7 while Indian gold prints a six-month low.

Four central banks, one mechanism: a war premium leaking out of crude and into core. The cross-border tell that matters to me as an operator in both Canada and India — weak CAD and weak INR are the same trade. Two dollar-sensitive, energy-exposed currencies getting crushed by a stronger dollar and a higher-for-longer path. This is a synchronized tightening regime, not a set of local decisions.

What happens next? Watch China. It has quietly cut crude imports by roughly 5 million barrels a day to cap the oil price. The day its reserves force it to buy again with Hormuz still shut is the day the "steady-state war" everyone is now pricing breaks. My call: the next surprise in oil is up, not down.

⚡ Signal Spotlight
China — not OPEC, not Iran — is setting the price of oil right now. It has cut crude imports by ~5 million barrels a day since the war began, and that single act of demand destruction is why Brent is near $95 instead of $130.
Beijing is drawing down strategic reserves to pull this off, which has a clock on it. The moment China resumes pre-war buying with the Strait still closed, you strip ~5% of global demand-side slack out of a supply-constrained market — and the price gaps. Every macro desk is modelling the war. Almost none are modelling China's reserve drawdown as the real swing factor. The risk is mispriced because it sits inside the one actor nobody can audit.

Macro · Europe↑now–year-endACT
The ECB raises rates for the first time since 2023 — turning the Hormuz energy shock into a central-bank problem, not just a crude-price one

The European Central Bank raised its three key rates by 25bp this morning — deposit to 2.25%, main refi to 2.40%, marginal lending to 2.65%, effective June 17 — its first hike since 2023. The Governing Council explicitly tied the move to the Middle East war and Strait of Hormuz disruption feeding energy-driven inflation, and called the decision "robust across a range of scenarios." New staff projections put euro-area HICP at 3.0% in 2026, easing to 2.3% in 2027, with growth a thin 0.8% this year.

SO WHAT: A central bank tightening into a supply shock is a signal about the durability of inflation, not the strength of demand. This converts the Hormuz war from a commodity event into a synchronized global tightening regime: ECB hiking, Fed futures now pricing a hike by year-end, the BoC holding but turning less dovish. For an operator with India and Canada exposure, the read-through is identical on both sides — a stronger dollar and a higher-for-longer path pressure both the rupee (95.7) and the loonie (USD/CAD 1.40, an 8-week low).
ACTION: Treat "higher for longer" as the base case across all four blocs through the summer. Stress-test floating-rate exposure and any thesis that quietly assumed H2 cuts. Watch the June 16–17 FOMC for whether the Fed validates the hike bets.
💭 Here is the version where this is wrong: the ECB just hiked at the exact moment oil is rolling over (WTI back below $90, China capping crude). If Hormuz reopens and energy falls, the ECB has tightened into the top — and will be cutting again by Q4, with this hike remembered as a policy error, not a regime.

The last time the ECB hiked (2022–23) it was chasing post-Covid and post-Ukraine inflation that it had called transitory for a year. The institutional memory of being late is precisely why it is moving pre-emptively now. The tell is in the projections: a 3.0% 2026 HICP that converges to 2.0% only by 2028 is the ECB conceding that energy-driven inflation is sticky. Note the divergence with Canada: the 10Y GoC is at ~3.49% versus the 10Y UST at 4.52% — nearly a full point of spread — which is exactly why the loonie keeps leaking against the dollar even as Canadian yields rise.

AI · Regulation · Europe↑precedent-settingNOTE
A German court makes Google the author of its own AI — Munich strips search "safe harbor" and holds it directly liable for false AI Overview answers

The Landgericht München I issued a preliminary injunction holding Google directly and primarily liable for false, defamatory claims generated by its AI Overviews, after the feature falsely tied two publishers to "dubious business practices" that appeared in none of its cited sources. The court ruled Google "alone has influence over the AI's offering and the algorithms" — making it the author and publisher, outside DSA host-provider protections and beyond notice-and-takedown.

SO WHAT: This is the first ruling to treat AI-generated output as the platform's own speech rather than third-party content it merely hosts. If it holds, every AI search and assistant product in Europe inherits publisher-grade liability for what its model says — a structural cost and design constraint, not a one-off fine. It reframes the entire "we just surface information" defense.
ACTION: Anyone shipping generative features into the EU should assume output liability is coming and build retrieval/citation guardrails now, before the precedent hardens on appeal.
💭 Here is the version where this is wrong: a preliminary injunction from one regional court is not settled law. Higher German and EU courts may restore intermediary protections, and the ruling becomes a footnote rather than a template.
Read original → Gmail source
This stacks on the Independent Publishers Alliance antitrust complaint and the European Commission's December 2025 Article 102 proceedings over AI Overviews using publisher content without compensation or opt-out. Europe is assembling a two-front pincer on AI search — liability for what the model says and antitrust for how it's built — that the US shows no appetite to match.
