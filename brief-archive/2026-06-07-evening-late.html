EDITOR'S READ
Suhail Seth · June 7, 2026

Friday the market told one story; this weekend the cap table told another. On Friday a $1 trillion chip rout said AI valuations had run too far ahead of cash. Then on June 5 Google quietly agreed to pay SpaceX $920 million a month — roughly $30 billion through 2029 — for access to about 110,000 Nvidia GPUs sitting in xAI's data centres, five days before SpaceX lists at a $1.77 trillion valuation. So which is true: AI is over-bid, or the largest buyers on earth are signing nine-figure monthly cheques to lock up compute they cannot build fast enough? Both. The equity multiple compressed; the demand for the underlying did not. That gap is the whole game right now.

The signal I keep coming back to is the one in the oil pit. On June 6, Iran sent attack drones at the Strait of Hormuz and fired seven ballistic missiles at Kuwait and Bahrain — a new escalation rung, two new Gulf targets — and the US answered by striking Iranian radar at Goruk and on Qeshm Island. A year ago that headline puts WTI through $100. Instead crude fell Friday and is sitting near $91. The war premium is being arbitraged away even as the shooting escalates. The market has decided Iran's Hormuz theatre is leverage, not a durable closure — and it is probably right, until it is catastrophically wrong for 48 hours.

For an operator in both my markets the through-line is the same: the cost of money is staying high (US jobs ran hot, the ECB is 99% set to hike June 11, the BoC holds Wednesday) and the cost of compute is becoming a financeable, contracted asset. Cheap capital is not coming back this half; cheap-ish, long-dated compute contracts are the new working capital. My forward call: the next company to copy Google's SpaceX deal — a hyperscaler signing a multi-year compute-offtake to fund someone else's build — will tell you more about 2027 than any Fed dot.

⚡ SIGNAL SPOTLIGHT
Iran just attacked two new Gulf states and oil went down. The market has stopped pricing the Hormuz tail — which is either the smartest call of the year or the most dangerous.
June 6 was the widest Iranian escalation in weeks: drones at Hormuz, ballistic missiles at Kuwait and Bahrain, US strikes on Goruk and Qeshm. Yet WTI closed Friday near $91, down ~3% on the day and holding only a +4% weekly gain. Traders have concluded that every Iranian move so far has been an "inspection" or a salvo, never a sustained physical closure — so the risk premium decays between incidents faster than each incident adds it. The mispricing is asymmetric: the downside of being wrong (a real >48-hour closure of 20% of seaborne oil) is a $20–30 gap, not a $3 one.
Time horizon: days to weeks — resolves on whether any incident converts to a sustained (>48h) closure.

The Lead
AI INFRASTRUCTURE ↑ now → 2029 WATCH
Google just agreed to pay $920M a month for AI compute — the demand signal that rebuts Friday's $1T chip rout

On June 5, the same day chip stocks lost about $1 trillion, Google agreed to pay SpaceX roughly $920 million per month from October 2026 through June 2029 — about $30 billion total — for access to ~110,000 Nvidia GPUs plus CPUs and memory housed at xAI's data centres. Google called it a response to "unexpected demand" for its new AI products. SpaceX disclosed it one week before listing on the Nasdaq under SPCX on June 12: ~556 million Class A shares at $135, a ~$75 billion raise at a $1.77 trillion valuation — the largest IPO ever. It follows an earlier Anthropic compute pact.

SO WHAT: This is the cleanest rebuttal to Friday's "AI is over-valued" tape. Equity multiples repriced on rates; the offtake market repriced upward. A hyperscaler signing multi-year, fixed monthly compute cheques to fund a rival's build is compute graduating into a contracted, financeable asset class — the thing futures exchanges have started circling. For operators, it reframes the AI capex debate from "is there a bubble?" to "who controls the GPUs, the power and the balance sheet to lock them up?"
ACTION: Watch the SPCX open Friday as a referendum on whether the chip rout was rates (recoverable) or demand (not). A strong open while semis are still bleeding confirms the demand-is-real read; a soft open says the multiple compression has spread to the infra layer itself.
💭 Here is the version where this is wrong: $920M/month to a pre-IPO issuer that also happens to be raising $75B looks less like arms-length demand and more like an ecosystem of cross-holdings (Google, xAI, SpaceX) inflating each other's books right before a listing. If "unexpected demand" softens, a $30B take-or-pay becomes a stranded-cost millstone, and the deal ages like a 1999 capacity swap.

GEOPOLITICS↑ liveWATCH
Iran widens the war — drones at Hormuz, missiles at Kuwait and Bahrain — and the US strikes Goruk and Qeshm, but the talks stay deadlocked

Early June 6, Iran launched attack drones toward the Strait of Hormuz and fired seven ballistic missiles at Kuwait and Bahrain, largely intercepted. The US said it had downed six Iranian one-way drones over two days and retaliated against Iranian coastal radar at Goruk and on Qeshm Island. An adviser to Iran's supreme leader told CNN talks are "at a deadlock" and "the ball is in Trump's court," demanding the US unfreeze billions in Iranian assets. The April ceasefire is, in practice, a fiction held together by the absence of a sustained closure.

SO WHAT: The new element is target expansion to Kuwait and Bahrain — Gulf states, not just shipping. That raises the odds of a wider regional response and a real (not theatrical) supply interruption. Yet oil still fell Friday, which means the market is offside if Tehran ever converts a salvo into a multi-day Hormuz shutdown. For energy-importing economies, the cheap-insurance window — buying oil-price protection while the premium is compressed — is open precisely because everyone has stopped fearing the tail.
ACTION: Treat WTI under $92 with active Hormuz escalation as an asymmetric hedging opportunity, not a calm. The trigger to watch is any transit interruption that lasts beyond an "inspection" window.
💭 The bear case for caring: Iran has cried wolf for two months. Each incident has reopened within hours; the US Navy escort posture caps duration; and Gulf air defence intercepted the Kuwait/Bahrain salvo. If the pattern holds, the correct trade was the one the market already made — fade the premium.

CANADA · AI↑5-yrNOTE
"AI for All": Carney's national strategy bets $200B of growth and 250,000 jobs on dragging adoption from 12% to 60%

Launched June 4, Canada's "AI for All" strategy promises new legislation, investment and programs over five years, targeting an extra $200B of economic growth and 250,000 AI jobs, and lifting AI adoption from just over 12% to 60% by 2034. It pairs with earlier-reported plans for a Canadian Tech Growth Fund and an AI Compute Access Fund.

SO WHAT: This is the Canadian bookend to today's Lead. While Google rents American compute by the month, Ottawa is trying to manufacture sovereign demand and capacity — cheap hydro power, critical minerals, federal anchor tenancy. The strategy is an adoption play, not a frontier-model play: Canada is betting it can win by deploying AI broadly rather than building the biggest lab.
ACTION: Watch for the dedicated dollar figure committed to the Tech Growth Fund and Compute Access Fund in the formal program detail — the difference between a framework and a cheque is the whole signal.
💭 Counter: 12%→60% adoption by 2034 is a heroic curve, and "$200B of growth / 250k jobs" is the kind of round number that rarely survives contact with a recession and a hostile US trade posture. Strategy documents are cheap; the compute and power build-out is where it lives or dies.

The Long Game
The quiet structural shift of 2026 is compute becoming a financed, contracted asset rather than a capex line. Google paying SpaceX $920M a month, Anthropic's earlier pact, exchanges circling AI-compute futures, and Canada designing a Compute Access Fund are all the same motion: GPUs and the power behind them are being turned into long-dated, creditworthy cash-flow streams. When that happens, control of compute starts to look like control of energy infrastructure a century ago — owned through offtake contracts and balance sheets, not bought on a spot market. The economies that learn to underwrite that asset class — not just consume it — are the ones that will set terms in the next cycle.
