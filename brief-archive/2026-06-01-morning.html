EDITOR'S READ
Suhail Seth · June 1, 2026

The market is pricing peace the battlefield hasn't signed — again. A 60-day memorandum to extend the Iran ceasefire and finally open nuclear talks pulled WTI back under $90 from the $106 spike of early May, and Trump still hasn't put his name to it. I've watched this loop since the war began February 28: every de-escalation headline knocks $10 off crude, every IRGC boarding puts it back. What's different now is the structure — a dated 60-day clock plus Rubio saying he'll give the talks "every chance to succeed." That's the closest thing to an exit ramp we've had.

Here's the cross-current nobody is drawing a line through: the same oil relief that let US indices print their ninth straight winning week is what's quietly hurting both of my home markets, not helping them. The Sensex shed 1,092 points Friday with the rupee pinned near 95.5 — the oil dividend is being eaten by a weak currency and foreign selling. Canada's loonie sits at 1.38 and the Bank of Canada stays frozen at 2.25% into June 10 precisely because oil-driven inflation hasn't fully cleared. Cheap crude is a US equity story and an EM/petro-currency drag in the same breath.

And underneath the geopolitics, the AI cycle just changed character. Anthropic at $965B now eclipses OpenAI's $852B, and both sprint toward fall IPOs. But The Ken's sharpest line this week — "the end of AI's venture era" — is the one I keep circling. When the labs go public, the markup party ends and the capital starts asking who actually deploys this. My call: the next dollar of AI alpha lives in the deployment and sovereign layer — HCL buying into Sarvam, Cohere standing up Canada's enterprise stack — not the model labs everyone is still valuing like seed-stage startups.

THE LEAD
GEOPOLITICS▼60-DAY CLOCKWATCH
Iran and Washington put a 60-day clock on the war — and crude falls back under $90 before Trump has even signed

Negotiators have agreed a 60-day memorandum of understanding to extend the ceasefire and begin formal talks on Iran's nuclear program — but the deal still needs President Trump's signature, and he has publicly insisted Iran will never control the Strait of Hormuz ("international waters, nobody's going to control it"). Secretary Rubio's line that the US will give talks "every chance to succeed" was enough to drop WTI more than 5% in a session; front-month WTI sat near $88.90 and Brent near $93.71 at Friday's close, down hard from the $106 mid-week spike of early May. Both benchmarks are still up 45%+ since the war began February 28.

SO WHAT: A dated framework changes the trade from "headline ping-pong" to a countdown with a knowable expiry — that is what lets equity desks fade the war. For a cross-border operator the asymmetry matters: cheaper crude flatters US multinationals and energy-importing manufacturers, but it lands on India and Canada through the currency, not the pump. India imports the barrel and pays in a 95-handle rupee; Canada exports the barrel and watches the loonie soften as the oil bid fades. The same $10 move helps one balance sheet and pressures another.
ACTION: Treat $80 WTI as the line that confirms the MOU is real, not theatre. Below it, lean into oil-sensitive importers and rate-cut-geared duration; a failed signing that re-spikes crude past $100 reverses both. Watch for the actual Trump signature — the market is already paid for it.
💭 The version where this is wrong: there is no signature, only a memo. Iran has used every prior "framework" as a stalling device while keeping the blockade leverage warm; a single tanker incident in the Strait re-arms a $20 risk premium overnight, and a market that pre-celebrated peace gets caught long and wrong.

The structural read: a 60-day MOU is a negotiating instrument, not a treaty. It buys both sides an off-ramp without forcing concessions — Iran keeps the implicit Hormuz threat in reserve, Washington keeps the naval posture. For oil, that means a capped-but-not-collapsed price: the risk premium compresses toward a floor (call it $80) but won't vanish while the blockade architecture exists. The cleanest expression of "deal holds" is not crude alone but the petro-currency pair: a rupee recovering through 94 and a loonie that stops softening would both confirm the premium is genuinely draining.

AI▲FALL IPOWATCH
Anthropic vaults past OpenAI at $965B — and the same week's warning is that "cheap AI" could sink the IPOs everyone's cheering

Anthropic closed a $65B Series H at a $965B valuation (Altimeter, Dragoneer, Greenoaks, Sequoia), eclipsing OpenAI's $852B March mark and making it the most valuable AI startup. SO WHAT: A $965B private mark on a business whose core output is deflating in price is the central tension of the cycle. The durable money won't be made re-rating the labs at the IPO — it's already paid for — but in the layer that monetises cheap intelligence: deployment, vertical agents, and sovereign/regulated stacks. That's the bridge from the US lab story to the India and Canada opportunities further down this issue.
ACTION: Use the fall IPO window as a sell-the-news risk marker for private AI markups, and a buy-the-build signal for deployment-layer names. Track inference $/token as the leading indicator of where margin is going.
💭 Where this is wrong: if frontier capability keeps outrunning commoditisation — each new model opening a new tier of tasks faster than the last one cheapens — the labs capture the deployment layer too, and $965B looks cheap in hindsight rather than peak.

💭 Where this is wrong: domestic absorption has limits. If FPI outflows accelerate on a stronger-for-longer dollar, retail SIP flows stop being a floor and become the bag-holder, and a 1,000-point day becomes a 5,000-point month.

THE LONG GAME
The slow-burn trend under everything in this issue is the migration of value down the AI stack — from the model labs valued at three-quarters of a trillion dollars toward the deployment, services, and sovereignty layer that actually delivers intelligence into regulated economies. India is playing it through its IT-services majors climbing up from labour arbitrage (HCL into Sarvam); Canada is playing it through a national champion rolling up allied sovereign-AI providers (Cohere absorbing Aleph Alpha). Same thesis, two geographies, one bet: when the capability commoditises, the durable margin belongs to whoever owns the wrapper, the compliance, and the customer relationship — not the weights. Watch inference price-per-token and government-anchor AI contracts as the two leading indicators of whether this migration is underway or just narrated.
