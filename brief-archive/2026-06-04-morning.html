EDITOR'S READ
Suhail Seth · June 4, 2026

For nine straight sessions the only direction was up. Yesterday the tape remembered there's a war on. A fresh exchange of strikes around the Strait of Hormuz — US forces hitting Qeshm Island, Iran firing ballistic missiles at its neighbours — pushed WTI up 2.4% to $96 and broke the S&P 500's nine-day winning streak.

What makes the day worth your attention isn't any single index; it's that one event punched all three of my economies through three different doors. In New York, the megacap-AI complex wobbled as the 10-year yield climbed to 4.46%. In Mumbai, the IT index cratered more than 5% — the rupee sits near ₹96, foreigners have sold for five sessions running, and an imported oil bill is the last thing an importer economy wants the week the RBI decides rates. In Toronto, the TSX handed back its brand-new 35,000 record.

But here's the asymmetry I keep returning to: Canada is the only one of the three that gets paid when oil spikes. A petro-linked currency and an energy-heavy index are a hedge, not a wound. The synchronized selloff hides a real divergence — India imports the shock, Canada exports it, and the US just pays more for money. One detail nobody flagged: the VIX actually fell, to 15.77. This was a controlled give-back, not a panic.

My call: if Trump's "Hormuz deal within a week" slips — and these always slip — $100 oil is the line that turns an orderly pullback into genuine risk-off. Watch the rupee, not the Dow.

GEOPOLITICS▼DAYS–WEEKSACT
Hormuz reignites — US strikes Qeshm Island, oil jumps to $96, and the S&P's nine-day record run snaps in a single session

A renewed round of strikes around the Strait of Hormuz ended the calm. US Central Command confirmed forces hit Qeshm Island in response to attempted Iranian attacks, while Tehran launched ballistic missiles toward neighbouring states. WTI settled up 2.41% at $96.02 and Brent at $97.81 — a third straight up-session. The S&P 500 fell 0.74% to 7,553.68, snapping a nine-day winning streak; the Dow shed 620 points (1.21%) and the Nasdaq lost 0.89%. President Trump said a memorandum to reopen the strait could land "within the next week," while conceding several issues remain unresolved.

SO WHAT An oil spike with no near-term ceiling is the cleanest macro stress test going: it lifts headline inflation, pushes back rate-cut timing, and firms the dollar — squeezing oil-importing economies and currencies while rewarding energy exporters. For a cross-border operator the read is directional — favour the petro-complex, stay cautious on import-margin and rate-sensitive names until the strait reopens.
ACTION Treat $100 WTI as the trigger line. Below it, this is an orderly pullback to add quality on. Above it, de-risk rate-sensitive and import-heavy exposure and assume the "deal within a week" has failed.
💭 Here's the version where this is noise: Trump's one-week MOU actually lands, the risk premium bleeds out as fast as it built in May (oil fell 20% from its 2026 peak late last month), and the streak resumes within days. The VIX falling to 15.77 says the smart money is already betting on exactly that.

May was the template: crude spiked to roughly $106 mid-month, then fell ~20% from its 2026 peak as US-Iran ceasefire optimism returned late in the month. The ceasefire has technically held "indefinitely" since April 21, but a US naval blockade and a contested Hormuz reopening remain the unresolved core. The strait carries close to a fifth of seaborne crude, which is why every flare-up reprices the global risk curve within hours. The tell to watch is duration: a one-day headline spike round-trips; a sustained closure above $100 is what forces central banks and importers to actually re-plan.

AI's borrowing binge meets a 4.5% world — hyperscalers may need $1.5 trillion in debt just as yields turn against them

Chartbook flagged it and the bond desks confirm it: the five largest hyperscalers issued about $121B of US corporate bonds in 2025, four times their prior annual average, and Wall Street pencils in roughly $300B of AI-related investment-grade supply for 2026. SO WHAT This is the quiet structural story beneath the AI rally: the capex is increasingly debt-financed, and that debt is priced off a 10-year yield that just rose to 4.46% on oil-driven inflation fear. A higher-for-longer curve lifts the hurdle rate on every data centre. Compute is becoming an asset class with a financing cycle — and that cycle now has a cost-of-capital problem.
ACTION Track hyperscaler net bond supply and IG spreads as a leading indicator of AI-capex sustainability, and watch whether any jumbo deal struggles to price.
💭 The bull case: these are the most cash-generative companies in history, demand for their paper is effectively bottomless, and a 4.5% coupon is trivial against AI's projected returns. The "debt-funded bubble" framing has been wrong at every prior rung of this cycle.

The mechanism is simple and under-appreciated: a data centre is a long-duration asset financed with long-duration debt, and long-duration debt is the single most rate-sensitive instrument there is. When the 10-year backs up 50bp on an oil-inflation scare, the present value of a 10-to-15-year build-out falls and the breakeven return rises. The cash-rich hyperscalers can absorb this; the second tier — neoclouds, GPU lessors, junk-rated AI infrastructure — cannot. That's where the financing cycle bites first, and it's the layer to watch if yields stay elevated through the back half of the year.

THE LONG GAME
The slow-burn story isn't oil and it isn't AI — it's the wire now connecting them. The AI build-out is increasingly financed in the investment-grade bond market, with the sector potentially needing well over a trillion dollars of new debt this decade. That debt is priced off the long end of the curve, and the long end is now hostage to a Middle East risk premium that refuses to resolve cleanly. For the first time, a tanker incident in the Gulf and a data centre in Texas share a single transmission mechanism: the 10-year yield. The next leg of the compute supercycle will be set less by model benchmarks than by the cost of capital — and the cost of capital now has a geopolitics problem. Watch the yield, because that is where the war and the boom finally meet.
