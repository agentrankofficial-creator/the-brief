EDITOR'S READ
Suhail Seth · June 10, 2026

For two months I've watched the tape treat the Iran war as a geopolitics story and an oil story. Today it stopped being either. US CPI printed 4.2% year-over-year for May — the hottest since April 2023 — with energy up a brutal 23.5%. China's factory-gate prices rose at their fastest pace since 2022. Japan's producer prices jumped. Three of the world's four biggest economies are now importing the same shock through the same chokepoint, and it has crossed from the pump into the core. The war premium didn't fade; it metabolised into inflation.

Here is the connection nobody is drawing cleanly: the market has, in a single week, flipped from pricing rate cuts to pricing rate hikes from both the Federal Reserve and the Bank of Japan. The synchronized global easing cycle that everyone built their 2026 book around is dead. And the cruel symmetry I keep coming back to — the rupee at ~95 and the loonie at ~0.718 are both being punished by the very same dynamic: a higher-for-longer dollar attached to an oil shock neither Mint Road nor Ottawa controls. India's fertiliser subsidy is now tracking to double its budget; Macklem walks into today's decision boxed by an inflation print he can't ease against.

So my call: stop trading the headlines from Hormuz and start trading the second derivative. The next leg isn't a missile — it's a fertiliser-and-food inflation wave that hasn't hit a single CPI basket yet. Who's positioned for a world where the Fed's next move is up?

⚡ SIGNAL SPOTLIGHT
The fertiliser that moved through Hormuz is the inflation shock that hasn't arrived yet — the food leg of this war is still in the post.
Roughly 30% of global fertiliser supply transited the Strait of Hormuz before the war; that flow is now choked. Brazilian farmers are already cutting purchases at a loss, India's fertiliser subsidy is tracking to double the budgeted figure, and the WEF is warning "the next food crisis is already in motion." Today's 4.2% CPI captured energy and shelter — it did NOT yet capture a planting season missed across the import-dependent global south. Food is the second shock, and it lands on a lag.
Time horizon: 2–3 quarters (Q3–Q4 2026 harvest & CPI prints)

The Lead
GLOBAL MACRO↑ ImmediateACT
US inflation roars back to 4.2% — and the market just buried the 2026 rate-cut trade for the Fed AND the Bank of Japan

May US CPI rose 4.2% year-over-year (and 0.5% on the month) — the highest reading since April 2023 — driven by a 23.5% surge in energy costs from the Iran war, with shelter at 3.4% and food at 3.1%. Core held at 2.9%. The same day, China's producer prices rose at their fastest pace since 2022 and Japan's PPI jumped (Japan imported ~90% of its oil from the Middle East pre-war). Traders now bet both the Fed and the BoJ will raise rates this year — a stunning reversal from the cuts priced just weeks ago. "Higher-for-longer energy means higher-for-longer inflation and lower-for-longer growth," an S&P economist warned.

SO WHAT: This is the first synchronized developed-market tightening impulse since 2022, and it's entirely energy-driven rather than demand-driven — the worst kind, because hiking into a supply shock buys disinflation only by crushing growth. For India: an RBI that was eyeing easing is now trapped, the rupee near 95 imports more inflation, and fuel/fertiliser subsidies balloon the fiscal. For Canada: the BoC's path to a cut evaporates just as a weak labour market needs relief — stagflation-lite in both jurisdictions, sourced from the same barrel of crude.
ACTION: Re-underwrite anything that assumed falling rates in H2 2026 — floating-rate debt, long-duration growth equity, rate-sensitive real estate. Favour pricing-power businesses and real assets over multiple-expansion bets. Watch the Fed's June 16–17 meeting as the live test.
💭 Here's the version where this is wrong: a single hot print driven by a war-energy spike is precisely what central banks are trained to "look through." If Israel-Iran de-escalation holds and oil keeps sliding (WTI already $87.73, gold at a year-low signalling fading fear), the energy base effect reverses by Q4 and the "hikes" repricing unwinds as fast as it arrived — making today's panic the top in yields, not the start of a cycle.

The mechanism that makes this dangerous is the breadth: energy (+23.5%) is the headline driver, but shelter ticking to 3.4% and food to 3.1% shows the pass-through is already moving into sticky core components. Markets pricing BoJ hikes is the real tell — Japan spent a generation fighting deflation; a BoJ forced to tighten by imported energy inflation would lift the yen, unwind carry trades, and tighten global dollar liquidity all at once. That is the channel through which a Middle East war reaches a Toronto mortgage and a Mumbai bond desk simultaneously.

GEOPOLITICS↑DaysWATCH
The Iran war just jumped its borders — strikes on US bases in three Gulf states and a Houthi threat to the Red Sea open a second chokepoint

Iran's military said it struck US bases in Bahrain, Jordan and Kuwait; Israel bombed Tyre in southern Lebanon (at least eight killed); and a cargo vessel off Yemen was attacked by a small boat after Houthi rebels threatened Red Sea shipping. With Hormuz traffic already choked, the Red Sea — 12% of global trade — is now the second pressure point. The widening complicates Trump's peace push, since Iran has made an Israeli withdrawal from Lebanon a precondition and Hezbollah has rejected Israel-Lebanon talks.

SO WHAT: A two-chokepoint war (Hormuz + Bab-el-Mandeb) is a fundamentally different supply-chain risk than a one-chokepoint war — it removes the Red Sea as the relief valve that absorbed earlier Hormuz disruption. For India, more cargo reroutes around the Cape, lengthening lead times on energy and fertiliser; for Canada, it keeps the global oil bid alive even as WTI dips, sustaining the inflation that pins the BoC.
ACTION: Track Red Sea transit/insurance rates as the leading indicator — they will move before oil does. Shipping and tanker names re-rate on a sustained Bab-el-Mandeb closure.
💭 The bear-on-escalation case: these are signalling strikes, not war-widening ones — calibrated to extract concessions before a deal, exactly as the April Hormuz "yo-yo" closures were. Oil falling to $87 says the market reads it that way.

MARKETS↓NowNOTE
Gold at a year-low while CPI runs hot — the cross-asset tell that says "rate hikes," not "inflation hedge"

Gold fell 1.76% to $4,286 — its lowest of 2026 — and silver hit a year-low too, even as CPI printed 4.2%. Bitcoin slid 2% to ~$62,500. On the surface a paradox: inflation up, the inflation hedge down. The resolution is rates: when the market prices central-bank hikes, rising real-yield expectations crush non-yielding assets faster than inflation lifts them.

SO WHAT: Gold and bitcoin falling on a hot CPI is the market voting that this is a real-rates story, not a debasement story. That's a clean signal for asset allocators in every market: the regime is "higher real rates," which favours cash and short-duration over both gold and long-duration growth — at least until the hike bets peak.
ACTION: Don't reflexively buy gold on the inflation headline. Watch real yields; gold turns when the hike-pricing exhausts, not when CPI prints.
💭 The gold bull's retort: a year-low into 4.2% CPI and a two-chokepoint war is exactly the capitulation that marks a bottom — if the Fed blinks and refuses to hike into a supply shock, gold reprices violently higher.

🍁 TRADE / INFRA↑Opens Jun 12NOTE
The Gordie Howe Bridge opens Friday — Canada built a $6.4B artery Trump publicly vowed to block, and won

The Gordie Howe International Bridge linking Windsor and Detroit opens Friday, with traffic flowing Monday. Canada funded the entire $6.4 billion project (tolls flow to Canada until costs are recovered, then split 50-50). It arrives two years late and ~$500M over budget — and in February Trump posted that he would "not allow" it to open until the US is "fully compensated." Ontario truckers could save up to $100,000 per fleet per month versus the rival Ambassador Bridge.

SO WHAT: The Windsor-Detroit corridor is the busiest Canada-US commercial crossing and the auto industry's spine. New capacity that lowers tolls is a direct, durable cost-down for Canadian cross-border logistics — and a quiet rebuke to the idea that a Truth Social post is policy. In a CUSMA-renewal year, a Canada-funded, Canada-controlled trade artery is leverage.
ACTION: Cross-border supply chains routing through Windsor should model the toll savings now; the Ambassador Bridge's pricing power just collapsed.
💭 The skeptic: a bridge two years late and half a billion over budget is not a triumph of execution — and tolls accrue to Canada only "until costs are paid off," which on $6.4B could be a very long time.

🌍 AFRICA↑StructuralWATCH
Congo turns its cobalt monopoly into a weapon against Beijing — as Rwanda becomes the first African economy to need an IMF lifeline since the war

The DRC's export caps on cobalt — it mines over 70% of global supply — sent smelters scrambling and pushed prices to record levels, a deliberate play to force domestic processing and exercise "leverage over China," the continent's biggest miner. SO WHAT: Africa is quietly repricing its raw-materials leverage at the exact moment the energy shock makes every input scarcer — cobalt for batteries, gas for LNG buyers fleeing Hormuz. ACTION: Stress-test battery/EV input costs for a sustained cobalt squeeze; watch whether other African producers copy the DRC's processing-mandate playbook.
💭 The bear: export caps invite substitution — cobalt-free LFP chemistries and recycling erode the DRC's leverage over time, and a country this aid-dependent can ill afford to choke its own export revenue for long.

The Long Game
Adam Tooze's Chartbook is pushing a frame worth holding onto: a "Tri-Polar World Spending Supercycle" in which public and private capital across AI, Defense and Climate surges in parallel across Asia, Europe and the Americas — roughly $10 trillion this year, forecast toward $16 trillion by 2030 (Gartner/Bloomberg estimates). It's the through-line connecting half of today's edition: Europe rearming and courting Korea's arms makers, Foxconn greening Vietnam, China building computing hubs, the IPO stampede funding AI infrastructure. The compute-as-an-investable-asset thread we've been tracking — AI energy demand becoming financialised — lives inside this. The investment implication is that "diversification across regions" no longer dilutes the bet, because all three poles are spending into the same three buckets at once. The risk: $16T of simultaneous AI+Defense+Climate spend, much of it deficit-funded, is also the structural force that keeps the inflation we led with today higher-for-longer. The supercycle and the rate scare are the same story told at different speeds.
