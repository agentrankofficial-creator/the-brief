EDITOR'S READ
Suhail Seth · May 25, 2026 · Morning

Kevin Warsh was sworn in as Fed chair on Friday in the East Room of the White House — the first time a Fed chair has been sworn in there since Alan Greenspan in 1987. Clarence Thomas administered the oath. Trump spoke at length. The optics were not subtle, and they were not meant to be. Janet Yellen and Jerome Powell were sworn in quietly at the Eccles Building by fellow governors. Warsh was sworn in under a chandelier, with the president of the United States standing two feet to his left talking about "greatness." That setting choice is not stagecraft. It is a policy signal.

And it lands into a bond market that was already broken before he picked up the gavel. The 30-year UST hit 5.197% on May 19 — a 19-year high — before settling back to 5.07%. Long bonds in the US, Japan, the UK and France all moved out of duration simultaneously last week. Barclays' Ajay Rajadhyaksha called it "get me out of duration" in four different political systems at once. The Iran-war fiscal repricing, the Bloom-Groshen estimate that the McEntarfer firing already cost the US economy $100B and 168,000 jobs by destroying confidence in BLS data, and the chronic G7 debt overhang are all pulling in the same direction. Warsh inherits a Fed where the long end is voting against the institution before he has held a single meeting.

Here is the cross-reference I think nobody is making yet. Anthropic's reported Microsoft Maia 200 talks (announced May 21, no deal yet) are the AI-cycle analogue of Warsh's swearing-in. Both are signals that a single point of leverage — Powell at the Fed, Nvidia in compute — is being engineered around because the institution can no longer be relied on to set the price. Anthropic now wants TSMC/Nvidia (training) + Amazon Trainium (Rainier) + Google TPU + Microsoft Maia 200 — four silicon sources for one model. That is the Larry Fink "compute as a futures category" thesis showing up not at the exchange yet, but on the procurement side first. If you are an AI lab, you are now hedging vendor risk the way a sovereign hedges currency risk. The Brief's compute-as-scarce-factor thesis is no longer aspirational — it is the operating regime.

India and Canada read this the same way. Both are EM-adjacent currencies tied to a Fed chair who has no room to either cut (oil shock + bond breakout) or hike (US labour market softening, eight straight S&P weekly gains masking real-economy stress). USD/INR closed 95.59 on Friday — recovered from the record-low 97.15 on May 19 only because RBI sold dollars all week and has a $5B 3-year swap auction announced for Tuesday May 26. USD/CAD touched 1.38195 the same day, on the same Dollar/oil dynamic. Both central banks have meetings inside the next 17 days (RBI swap settlement May 26, BoC June 10). Both have to decide whether to defend the currency, defend growth, or accept that under a politicised Fed they don't get to pick.

What I am watching for next: Warsh's first FOMC is June 16-17. The market is already speculating he may have to hike, not cut, despite Trump bringing him in explicitly for cuts. If that's how it plays — Warsh raising rates from the chair Trump installed him in 25 days earlier — that is the cleanest possible signal that the post-2008 central-bank-as-policy-tool era is over. The new regime is: compute, oil, debt, and the dollar all repriced against political control. Anthropic, RBI and BoC are running the same hedging playbook on a different axis each.

Warsh sworn in at the White House — the first Fed swearing-in there since Greenspan in 1987 — and the bond market broke before he picked up the gavel

Kevin Warsh took the oath of office Friday May 22 from Supreme Court Justice Clarence Thomas in the East Room of the White House, replacing Jerome Powell as chair of the Federal Reserve. Trump gave lengthy political remarks at the ceremony. The Senate confirmed Warsh 54-45 on May 13 on a near-party-line vote; only Senator John Fetterman (D-PA) crossed over. The last Fed chair sworn in at the White House was Alan Greenspan, four decades ago. The two most recent chairs were sworn in quietly at the Eccles Building. Warsh's first FOMC is June 16-17. He inherits a 30-year UST that hit a 19-year high of 5.197% just three days before his ceremony, and a synchronised four-country (US/UK/Japan/France) long-bond unwind that Barclays' Ajay Rajadhyaksha called "get me out of duration" in four political systems at once.

SO WHAT: The setting choice — White House, presidential remarks, partisan oath-giver — is itself the policy signal: Fed independence is now a contested rather than assumed condition. For Indian importers and Canadian exporters, the implication is the same: the Dollar floor is being set by political dynamics, not data dynamics. INR/CAD vol gets structurally higher. For a multi-jurisdiction operator, currency hedge cadence shortens from quarterly to monthly.
ACTION: Watch the June 16-17 FOMC for the first dissent count. Three or more dissents is the regime-change signal. A unified hold or cut means Warsh has consolidated; a fragmented decision means the Fed is now operating like the ECB at its worst.
💭 The bear case: Warsh has historically been a hawk, has criticised QE since 2010, and is on record opposing the Powell rate cuts of late 2024. If he comes in and holds — or hikes — that is not "politicisation," it is the most orthodox Fed chair in 25 years correcting an over-easy stance. The optics are political; the policy may be the cleanest restoration of pre-Bernanke central banking the market has seen.

The institutional context: Bloom-Groshen-Hobbs-Strain published a working paper this week estimating the August 2025 firing of BLS chief Erika McEntarfer cost the US economy $100B+ in GDP and 168,000 non-farm payrolls — quantified through a 50% jump in the Economic Policy Uncertainty index in the week after the firing. The Fed Warsh inherits has a credibility problem on data inputs before he even gets to the rate decision.
