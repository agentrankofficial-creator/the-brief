The Brief

The Pope warned AI must be disarmed — the FT showed how to undo it in 10 minutes. India drafts a 2,000-km pipeline to escape Hormuz. Canada hires its own teenagers back.
EDITOR'S READ
Suhail Seth · May 26, 2026 · Evening

Two AI stories arrived within 24 hours of each other, and they answer each other so precisely it almost feels staged. Yesterday at Vatican Synod Hall, Pope Leo XIV launched his first encyclical, Magnifica Humanitas, and demanded AI be "disarmed" — his deliberate, weaponised verb — and prevented from becoming "an instrument of domination, exclusion, and death." This morning, the Financial Times published an investigation, in partnership with the AI safety group Alice, showing that off-the-shelf software — the team used a GitHub tool called Heretic — can strip the safety guardrails from Meta's Llama 3.3 and Google's Gemma 3 in roughly ten minutes on a laptop. The technique is called abliteration, and the models, once stripped, will obligingly explain how to disperse chlorine gas in a crowd. The Pope identifies the threat. The FT demonstrates the threat is already operational.

This is the gravitational center of today's edition, and it pulls four other threads with it. First: the Cohere Command A+ Apache 2.0 release Suhail's brain has been celebrating as the sovereign-AI proof-of-concept now wears a different colour — open weights are also abliteration-vulnerable weights, and the very feature that lets a Bay Street bank or an Indian PSU run a frontier model on-premise is the feature that strips safety in minutes. The closed-weight versus open-weight debate has stopped being a procurement question and become a safety regime question. Second: the Vancouver protests Saturday against Telus's two AI data centres (750 marchers) and the parallel grumble in Mumbai about water-and-power data-centre approvals are not just NIMBYism — they are the physical-infrastructure echo of the same suspicion the encyclical names. Third: India's revisited ₹40,000 crore Middle East–India Deepwater Pipeline (MEIDP) shows a country trying to physically escape geopolitics, the way the abliteration story shows the safety regime trying to escape mathematics. Both are exits from a structure that can no longer be patched in place. And fourth: Jensen Huang's blunt "we've largely conceded China to Huawei" quote on CNBC last week — confirmed by Beijing-Washington talks resuming on AI governance over the weekend — closes the loop. The two-superpower AI bifurcation that the Pope wants disarmed is, on the silicon side, already permanent.

I think the call from here is unsentimental: open-weight is the next regulatory front, not sovereign-AI's victory parade. The window in which Cohere's open release reads as pure Canadian sovereignty win is short — three to nine months — before the OECD/Ottawa/Delhi conversation pivots to "which weights are allowed to leave a jurisdiction, and what abliteration-resistant constitutional baking has to happen first." Closed-weight + sovereign-foundry stack quietly becomes the more durable thesis. Watch which government cites Magnifica Humanitas by name in primary legislation first — that's the leading indicator of the regime forming around it. I'd guess the EU AI Act amendment process by Q4 2026.

⚡ SIGNAL SPOTLIGHT
India's ₹40,000 crore Oman subsea gas pipeline is not an infrastructure project — it is the first serious attempt by a major economy to physically opt out of the Hormuz risk-premium altogether, and it inverts what counts as an "energy hedge." The next generation of trade infrastructure won't reduce dependency on geopolitics; it will route around geography.
The Middle East–India Deepwater Pipeline (MEIDP) is a 2,000-km undersea corridor running at depths up to 3,450 metres from Oman's coast to Gujarat, carrying 31 mmscmd of natural gas at full capacity. South Asia Gas Enterprise has already completed seabed surveys — a sunk cost that signals real intent, not vapourware. The strategic detail buried in the Finshots reporting is the long-term tap-in plan: UAE, Saudi, Qatar, Turkmenistan and ultimately Iran could feed gas into the same physical artery. India consumes 190 mmscmd today, projected at 365 mmscmd by 2030, with zero strategic gas reserves to buffer a price shock. Connect this to the seventh Hormuz head-fake we've watched since April — Iran said "deal not imminent" Monday morning, US strikes hit southern Iran early Tuesday, WTI fell anyway — and the political logic is clear. Tankers can reroute; that's their virtue. Pipelines cannot; that's their bet. India is making the bet that a 5-to-7-year subsea pipe ages better than another decade of Iranian regime volatility. The same instinct is visible in the UAE quitting OPEC to deepen its AI/energy nexus, in Cohere's open-weight release as a sovereignty hedge, in Anthropic's four-vendor compute stack. The vocabulary of the next decade is physical-routing-around-politics. Operators who can supply or service deepwater pipeline infrastructure (subsea pipelay vessels, ROV inspection, deep-water welding, corrosion-resistant alloys, GAIL/EIL/IOC supply chain) are positioned for a structural decade tailwind from this thesis — not just one project.
Time horizon: 5–7 years to first gas; 12–18 months for the operator-stack re-rate

THE LEAD

AI · SAFETY ↓ Regulatory inflection · 3–9 months ACT
Pope demands AI be "disarmed" — and within 24 hours the FT shows how to actually do it in ten minutes on a laptop, using a GitHub tool called Heretic on Meta's Llama 3.3 and Google's Gemma 3

Pope Leo XIV launched his first encyclical Monday at Vatican Synod Hall, titled Magnifica Humanitas, calling for AI to be "disarmed" and "freed from logics that turn it into an instrument of domination, exclusion, and death." Anthropic co-founder Chris Olah shared the stage. Tuesday morning the Financial Times, partnering with AI safety group Alice, published the operational counterpoint: a technique called abliteration — implemented in an off-the-shelf GitHub tool called Heretic — analyses an open-weight model's internal refusal-direction vectors and edits those weights out. Tested on Llama 3.3 and Gemma 3, the stripped models cheerfully explained credit-card-stealing malware and dispersing chlorine gas in a crowd. Microsoft's parallel research earlier this year defeated guardrails on 15 named models with a single prompt. Google's official statement to FT: "abliteration is a known technical challenge facing all open models."

SO WHAT: The open-weight versus closed-weight debate has stopped being a procurement question and become a safety regime question. Every government writing AI legislation now has a clean demonstration that the safety claims of Llama, Gemma, Mistral, DeepSeek and Cohere can be undone with off-the-shelf tooling. Closed-weight providers (Anthropic, OpenAI, xAI) just acquired a material regulatory moat without lifting a finger. For India, where MeitY just signalled the IndiaAI Mission would lean on open-weight foundations, this is a real strategic problem: sovereignty and on-premise control come bundled with abliteration-vulnerability. For Canada, the Cohere Command A+ Apache 2.0 release celebrated last week as sovereign-AI victory now carries a footnote — open weights are also abliterable weights, and Ottawa's procurement narrative needs new safety guarantees.
ACTION: Watch the EU AI Act amendment process (next window: Q3 2026) for the first regulatory citation of Magnifica Humanitas. Map which open-weight providers (Cohere first; Mistral and Meta next) ship "abliteration-resistant" constitutional-baking research within 90 days. Closed-weight API providers re-rate quietly; open-weight pure plays carry a new regulatory tail.
💭 The contrarian read: the open-weight community has been doing this for two years and the sky has not fallen. Bad actors who want chlorine recipes already have access to Hugging Face, Tor, and the open internet. The Pope is moralising; the FT is sensationalising; nothing about the actual threat landscape changed today. The real loser isn't open-weight — it's the safety-research industrial complex, because abliteration shows alignment training is a thin lacquer rather than structural integrity, and Anthropic's interpretability stack is the only frame that survives.

AI · CHINA ↓ Structural WATCH
Jensen Huang says Nvidia has "largely conceded" China's AI chip market to Huawei — the 20% data-center revenue line that ran through Beijing is now structurally zero, and the bifurcation is permanent

In a CNBC interview last Thursday picked up across the weekend press, Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang acknowledged the company has "largely conceded" China's advanced AI chip market to Huawei. China previously accounted for roughly 20% of Nvidia's data center revenue; Huang now describes the company as "largely shut out." On H200 approvals he told investors to "expect nothing." Over the same weekend, Beijing confirmed bilateral AI-governance talks with Washington following the Trump-Xi meeting — a parallel diplomatic track that quietly accepts the silicon bifurcation as fait accompli. Huawei's Ascend line has gone from "punchline to production" in roughly three years.

SO WHAT: The two-stack AI world is now a publicly conceded fact, not a market hypothesis. Operators selling into both jurisdictions have to build dual silicon supply chains, dual safety regimes, dual data residency. The Tau Scaling Law / LogicFolding architecture announcement out of Huawei earlier this week wasn't a stunt — it was the public proof that "good enough" Chinese silicon for the domestic market exists. The competitive question for Nvidia shifts to whether the rest of the world (India, Gulf, Latin America, ASEAN) remains durably Nvidia-only, or whether Huawei's price-performance crosses into procurement at the second tier. For Indian PSU AI procurement that has so far been Nvidia-default, Huawei Ascend at a 30–40% price discount becomes the procurement question of late 2026.
💭 The Nvidia bear case here is that "conceded" reads as a CEO managing expectations down before a quarter, not a long-term strategic call. If a US-China trade détente lands and H200 export approval reopens, Nvidia is at the door already, and Huawei's three-year head start in domestic deployment is suddenly less defensible.

INDIA · MONETARY → Days–weeks WATCH
RBI executes its $5B 3-year USD/INR buy-sell swap auction today — the operation injects ~₹42–43,000 Cr of durable rupee liquidity and the size signals regime-management, not crisis-fighting

The Reserve Bank of India today conducted the $5B 3-year buy-sell USD/INR swap auction announced May 20. Near-leg settles May 29; far-leg matures May 29, 2029. The operation injects approximately ₹42,000–43,000 crore of durable banking-system liquidity, addressing the rupee-defense-induced cash tightness that built through May. USD/INR pierced 96.96 on May 20 close — the historic low — and has stabilised in the high-95s since the announcement. The forward premium term structure flattened modestly into the auction.

SO WHAT: The relevant question isn't whether the auction succeeded — RBI swap auctions of this size routinely clear — but where forwards settle post-auction. A flat-to-tight forward premium across the 3-year tenor confirms that the RBI is comfortable supplying USD into the curve at this level, which implies the implicit USD/INR ceiling for 2026 is in the 96-97 zone. For Indian importers, that's a hedging anchor. For exporters, the rupee weakness re-rate that has driven the IT services upside since March stays in place. For Suhail's cross-border thesis: an INR ceiling around 96 with a CAD/USD ceiling around 1.38–1.40 is the FX backdrop you size sensitively-EM positions to, not the one you trade against.
💭 Big swap auctions can also signal that organic capital inflows are weaker than they should be. If foreign portfolio flows remain net-out and the trade deficit re-widens on Hormuz-linked oil through Q3, the RBI may find itself running a second auction by August — and at that point the "regime management" frame starts looking like a slow-motion defense.

AI · INEQUALITY ↑ 2–5 years NOTE
Rest of World names the "agentic divide" — companies with high-quality, trustworthy AI agents will compound advantage faster than the gap between desktop-haves and have-nots ever produced

Rest of World's lead this week, citing King's College London digital economist Nick Srnicek, frames AI agents not as productivity tools but as a new axis of structural inequality. The framework: "new inequalities of access, scale, quality and trust — divides between those who have agents and those who don't; those who have good agents and those who have bad agents; those who have many agents and those who have few agents; those who can trust their agents and those who cannot." Same issue documents Africa's struggle for AI sovereignty against Big Tech infrastructure lock-in, Samsung labour conflict in South Korea over AI profit-sharing, and the UAE's OPEC exit explicitly to free oil revenue for AI infrastructure spend.

SO WHAT: The agentic-divide thesis is the moral-economy companion piece to the abliteration story above. Both name the same structural fact — frontier capability is concentrating in a few well-resourced labs, and the gap to the second tier widens faster than open-weight diffusion compensates for. For India and Canada, the policy question shifts from "build your own LLM" to "ensure agent-quality, agent-trust and agent-scale access for your domestic SME base before the gap is uncloseable." Both governments are early in this conversation; the operators ahead of the policy curve (Wealthsimple, Cohere, Inc42's "Forward-Deployed Engineer" hiring boom) are the ones to track. The UAE OPEC exit is the most striking signal — when a Gulf petrostate explicitly trades oil cartel discipline for AI infrastructure capex, you know the energy/AI compound has crossed a threshold.
💭 The agentic-divide framing assumes agents themselves become the dominant work substrate. If the next 24 months show agent reliability staying stuck at the 60–80% task-completion ceiling — as Alibaba's Qwen agent trial and most enterprise pilots have so far — then the divide is between people who pay for agent-mediated services and people who pay humans for the same task, which is exactly the inequality structure we already had. New label, same problem.

🍁 CANADA · LABOUR ↑ 3–12 months WATCH
Tim Hortons commits to hiring 10,000 local workers and explicitly stops lobbying Ottawa to expand the Temporary Foreign Worker program — the most lobbied corporate user of the TFW program just blinked, and the political ceiling on TFWs has effectively been set

Restaurant Brands International, Tim Hortons' parent, announced today a campaign to hire 10,000 Canadian workers, alongside an explicit pledge to stop lobbying the federal government to expand the TFW program. Tims says it has cut TFW usage by 50% since 2024; current TFW share is ~4,000 workers, or 3.6% of restaurant roles. The chain plans to build 80 new locations and renovate 400 by end-2027, with $130M direct investment from RBI and $270M from franchisees. For Canadian operators with TFW-dependent workforces (Loblaw, McDonald's Canada, A&W, Sobeys, agriculture, hospitality), the cost-side reset is now real: 14% youth unemployment is a hiring buyer's market, wages settle higher than the TFW counterfactual but lower than 2024 panic peaks, and operating margins compress 50–150 bps in low-wage retail and hospitality through 2026. 💭 "Stop lobbying to expand" is not the same as "stop using." Tims keeps 4,000 TFWs in role, the 10,000-hire target is over an 18-month window during which it's adding 80 stores, and the net-net reduction in TFW headcount is closer to 1,500 than 4,000. The corporate-virtue gesture is real; the labour-market re-pricing implication is smaller than the headline.

🍁 CANADA · POLITICS ↓ 5 months to ballot WATCH
Carney calls Alberta's separation-precursor referendum a "very dangerous bluff" and pulls his Bank of England Brexit experience directly into the framing — the federal-vs-province gloves are off five months before the October ballot

PM Mark Carney, in remarks Monday and Tuesday in Ottawa, labelled Alberta Premier Danielle Smith's referendum on holding a future binding separation referendum a "very dangerous bluff" and explicitly invoked Brexit, the decision he watched play out as governor of the Bank of England in 2016. His core argument: separation votes get framed as "free options" by advocates but end up locking in damage that takes a decade to undo. The Smith government has put the precursor question on the October 19, 2026 ballot. Saskatchewan Premier Scott Moe simultaneously urged Alberta to "look ahead, not separate."

SO WHAT: The federal-provincial register has changed. Through April, the Carney–Smith MoU on the Pathways oil-sands pipeline was the institutional frame. The Brexit invocation moves the conversation into the "national-unity emergency" register, which is a leveraged position on both sides — Carney signals federal seriousness, Smith counter-signals provincial agency. The CAD market response will be muted until polling tightens, but if October polling shows the precursor question failing by less than 20 points (vs the 30+ point margin I expect from our standing call on this), expect a structural CAD risk-premium of 50–80 bps to develop into Q4. The 10-year GoC at 3.45% has zero of this priced in today. The Pathways pipeline timeline is the variable to watch: if Carney can sign and break ground on a major TMX-style federal-backed pipeline before fall, the separation question loses its biggest economic grievance and the vote underperforms.
💭 The Brexit comparison is a category error. Brexit was a binding referendum on a clear yes/no question with a constitutional exit mechanism. Alberta's October vote is a referendum on whether to hold a future referendum — two layers of insulation, no constitutional exit path, and no precedent that "leverage" referendums actually generate concessions in Canadian federalism. THE LONG GAME

📈 THE LONG GAME
The most under-discussed structural shift across today's stories is what I'll call physical-routing-around-politics. India is planning a 2,000-km subsea pipeline because Hormuz cannot be diplomatically fixed in a timeframe shorter than the pipeline build. The UAE quit OPEC to free oil revenue for AI infrastructure that bypasses the cartel discipline it used to depend on. Anthropic disclosed a four-vendor compute stack to bypass Nvidia single-supplier risk. Cohere released open-weight to bypass closed-API geopolitical exposure. Vancouver protesters are organising to bypass the federal AI infrastructure consensus locally. Each of these is a different operator making the same structural call: the friction of fixing the system through political alignment is now higher than the cost of physically routing around it. The 2010s thesis was globalisation and just-in-time. The 2020s thesis is geographic redundancy. The 2030s thesis — the one being committed to right now in feasibility studies and capex plans — is physical-routing-around-politics, and it is going to be more capital-intensive than the prior decade by a factor of three. Long the pickaxes: subsea infrastructure, HVDC interconnects, distributed power generation, multi-jurisdiction data centre operators, sovereign-fab capacity, model-deployment integrators, container shipping route optionality. Short the assumption that any one supply chain stays where it is.
