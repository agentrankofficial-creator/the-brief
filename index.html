⬥ Editor's Read
Suhail Seth · June 9, 2026

One hundred days in, the Iran war stopped being an event and became a regime. Last night Iran and Israel both halted strikes — but only after Israel's air force hit the Mahshahr petrochemical complex and Iran warned it would restart the moment a bomb lands in Lebanon. That is not peace. That is a thermostat. Oil now carries a standing $10–15 risk premium that neither a ceasefire nor a strike fully discharges: WTI jumped 4% to above $94 on the exchange, and a halt won't take it back to $80. I've stopped trading the headlines and started pricing the floor.

The deeper tell is in the currencies. The same hot-US-jobs, higher-for-longer dollar that crushed gold to a three-month low Friday is hammering both the rupee (95.68) and the loonie (0.717) — two petro/EM-linked currencies taking the identical beating from one Fed dynamic. If you operate in India and Canada, as I do, you're short the same trade twice. And then Europe handed us the year's clearest structural signal: Germany and France killed FCAS, the $110B sixth-generation fighter, because Airbus and Dassault couldn't agree who builds the wings. Existential security threat, €100B on the table, and Europe still couldn't pool one program.

Here's the through-line nobody's drawing: alliances are fragmenting (US-Europe, France-Germany) at the exact moment capital is concentrating (SpaceX wants $1.77T Friday). My forward call: the BoC holds at 2.25% tomorrow and blames energy — the real question is whether Macklem hints the next move is a hike. If he does, the loonie finds its floor, and the cheapest petro-currency in the G7 becomes a buy.

⚡ Signal Spotlight
The death of Europe's fighter jet is a preview of how every European "sovereign AI compute" dream dies — on the exact France-vs-Germany workshare fault line.
FCAS didn't collapse on money or politics; it collapsed because France insisted on nuclear-capable, carrier-launched specs and refused to share the IP, while Germany wanted two aircraft under one umbrella. That is the precise dynamic — who hosts, who owns the model weights, who gets the sovereign carve-out — that will fracture the next "EuroStack" or "European AI gigafactory" consortium. Watch the next continental compute announcement split along the identical seam: national champions will not surrender workshare even with an existential case on the table.

Geopolitics→days–weeks WATCH
Iran and Israel both holster their missiles on day 100 — but only after Israel torches a petrochemical complex and Iran makes Lebanon the new tripwire

After the first direct missile exchange since the April truce, both sides halted strikes Monday — Iran announced the pause in the afternoon, Israel after its air force completed an "extensive strike against strategic defence systems" and hit the petrochemical complex at Mahshahr in southwest Iran. Tehran's condition was explicit: attacks resume if Israel keeps bombing Lebanon. Trump told the Financial Times both sides are "looking to do an immediate ceasefire" and insisted the flare-up won't derail talks. WTI spiked ~4% to above $94 intraday; Asian equities cratered (Nikkei −3.85%, Kospi as much as −8.8%) before US markets rebounded.

SO WHAT: The market has repriced this conflict from tail risk to standing condition. For a global operator that means oil's floor has moved up, not its ceiling — the durable mechanism is a permanent Gulf risk premium, which keeps petro- and EM-currencies (INR, CAD) structurally pressured against a higher-for-longer dollar. Both an India operator and a Canada operator are exposed to one variable: a Strait that never fully reopens.
ACTION: Treat $88–96 WTI as the new base case, not a spike. Hedge input-cost exposure on that band rather than betting on a clean ceasefire resolution. The Lebanon clause is the most likely re-ignition trigger before any Iran proposal lands.
💭 Here's the version where this is wrong: the mutual halt is the real turn, Trump lands a framework within two weeks, the Strait genuinely reopens, and oil mean-reverts toward $80 — at which point the "permanent premium" thesis looks like recency bias and the petro-currency shorts unwind hard.

Day-100 ledger (Al Jazeera/CNBC): the war saw 39 days of active US-Israel-Iran fire (Feb 28–Apr 7), then a fragile ceasefire from April 8 that never delivered a Hormuz reopening. The pattern across 100 days is a roughly 10-day "Hormuz yo-yo" of closure/seizure events — which is exactly why a single halt doesn't reset the premium. The Mahshahr strike is notable because it's economic-infrastructure targeting, not just military sites: it raises the cost of any Iranian re-escalation by threatening export capacity.

EUROPEDefence↓structuralWATCH
Europe's $110B fighter jet is officially dead — Macron and Merz kill FCAS because Airbus and Dassault couldn't agree who builds what

Germany and France scrapped FCAS, the sixth-generation combat aircraft launched in 2017 (later joined by Spain), at >$110B the most expensive European defence program ever attempted. France demanded nuclear-capable, carrier-launched specs and refused to share IP; Germany's two-aircraft compromise was rejected. Only the "Combat Cloud" networked component survives; the crewed jet is abandoned. Merz and Macron concluded at an EU-Western Balkans summit there was no path through the Airbus-Dassault deadlock.

SO WHAT: This is the year's sharpest evidence that Europe cannot pool industrial capacity even under an existential security threat with €100B+ on the table. The mechanism — national champions refusing workshare — generalises directly to every European attempt at sovereign compute, chips, or AI infrastructure (see today's Signal Spotlight). Fragmentation is the European default, not the exception.
ACTION: Discount European "joint sovereign" industrial announcements heavily; assume they fracture on workshare. Watch whether France goes national with Dassault.
💭 Wrong-case: the collapse is the shock that forces a leaner, faster bilateral or UK-Italy-style consortium (GCAP) to absorb the talent — Europe rearms better without the political baggage of FCAS.

The Long Game
The slow-burn trend under everything this week is concentration vs. fragmentation running in opposite directions at once. Capital is concentrating to a degree without modern precedent — a single private company asking for $1.77T in one listing, regional VC pooling 92% into one city-state, frontier AI renting out to even Apple. Meanwhile the cooperative structures of the last 70 years are fragmenting — US-Europe defence trust, the France-Germany industrial axis, the Western alliance's ability to build one jet together. The investable question for the next several years isn't "AI or no AI." It's whether a world where money concentrates and alliances fracture is stable enough to keep the concentration trade going — or whether fragmentation eventually reaches the capital markets themselves. Watch the sovereign-compute consortiums: they're where these two trends collide first.
