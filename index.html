Three things flipped today that the morning Brief got wrong, and one important thing didn't. First, Bill Bishop's reading from Tuesday — Jensen Huang is not on the plane to Beijing — was refuted by the time Air Force One touched down: Jensen is in the delegation alongside Musk, Cook and Fink. The "no Jensen = no chip deal" framing is dead within twenty-four hours of being written. Second, India did not just appeal — it acted. The government raised the gold and silver import duty to 15% before market open, turning Monday's twin appeals into a concrete policy step ten days inside this Brief's own forecast window. Third, Indian benchmarks broke their four-day losing streak — Sensex eked +0.07% to 74,609, Nifty +0.14% to 23,413, Nifty Metal led at +3% — a domestic value-buying rotation, not a foreign-flow reversal.

The fact that did not flip is the one that matters: USD/INR still printed 95.77, fresh record-low territory, on a day when both the duty hike and the equity recovery were visible. The policy did not stabilise the currency. The rotation did not reverse foreign outflows. Bank Nifty closed -99 even as Nifty Metal ripped. The bid is moving inside India, not into it.

What this sets up for Friday's joint communiqué from Beijing is unusually clean. Jensen physically present means a chip arrangement of some shape is now plausible — even if narrow and technical. The market is pricing the possibility of Iran/Hormuz de-escalation language as a hedge, which is why metals led: a Hormuz-resolution path is the only mechanism that compresses Brent back below $90 and unblocks India's monsoon-import math. If Friday's communiqué contains the words, INR rallies hard from 95.77 within five sessions. If it doesn't, today's domestic-rotation bounce gets erased before Monday open. The Cerebras pricing tonight at the top of $150-$160 is the AI-compute demand end of the same trade — the IPO of the year prints the same 24 hours the head of Nvidia is negotiating the supply side in Beijing. Both ends of the chip flow are being financialised on the same day.

BLS reported yesterday that April YoY CPI hit 3.8%, up from 3.3% in March and 2.4% in February (before the US-Israel strike on Iran). Annual wage growth slowed to 3.6%. THE LONG GAME
⌬ SLOW BURN
Foreign ownership of Indian equities falling below domestic institutional ownership for the first time is a regime change that nobody is calling a regime change yet. For two decades, the dominant theory of Indian capital markets has been that FIIs set the marginal price and DIIs absorb. That has now flipped. What it means in practice — over a 5-10 year horizon — is that Indian valuations stop tracking US-Treasury-yield arithmetic and start tracking domestic SIP-flow seasonality. Every macro book that priced in the old regime has to be re-written. The most contrarian trade for the next decade isn't "buy India when it's cheap" — it's "stop using foreign benchmarks to define when India is cheap." That re-rating starts with sectors that DIIs structurally over-own: financials, consumer staples, and PSU-adjacent capex plays. The ones FIIs over-owned — IT services, midcap tech, and select pharma — are going to drift in a different gravity field for years.
