EDITOR'S READ
Suhail Seth · May 10, 2026 (Evening)

Goldman put a number on it this week and the number is $7.6 trillion — total cumulative AI infrastructure capex from 2026 through 2031, scaling from $765B this year to $1.6T annually by 2031. That's a chip-and-data-center bill bigger than the entire global oil-and-gas capex of the past decade. The instinct is to read this as a US-China story. I think that's the trap. The interesting question — for me, sitting between Bombay and Bay Street — is which corners of this build flow toward markets that aren't called by their first names in the report.

India this week did its part: Skyroot crossed unicorn status with $60M from GIC and BlackRock-managed funds — the first time a private Indian company has built rockets from scratch and got priced at $1.1B by global allocators. Same week, Paytm posted its first-ever full-year profit (₹552 Cr) — though I'd note the ₹854 Cr of "other income" is still doing more work than the core. India is now both deeptech-credible and finally producing platform-company profitability. That's a structural inflection, not a quarterly headline.

Canada is the harder read. The April jobs print on Friday was bad — minus 18,000 jobs, jobless rate at 6.9% (six-month high), full-time down 47k, and YTD the worst four-month run since the pandemic. WTI sat at $95.42 with the Iran-UAE exchange of fire — so Canada has weak labour AND oil-driven inflation pressure. That's stagflation lite, and it lands two weeks after the BoC held at 2.25% and three weeks before the next StatsCan print. Carney's stuck. He told CBC last week he's not "chasing a small deal" with Trump — fine — but the structural answer is the one nobody is making out loud yet. Canada has cheap hydro, critical minerals, sovereign-friendly capital, and a cratering jobs market. The $7.6T AI build needs power and minerals at scale. The trade Carney should be making isn't auto/aluminum tariff relief — it's positioning Canada as the AI infrastructure backbone for the West. Quebec's hydropower + Alberta's gas + Ontario's nuclear (Bruce Power moving on 4,800 MW) + critical-minerals exports to fab-state US partners is a real industrial strategy. He has 90 days to announce one before the next BoC meeting forces a different conversation.

The trade for me: India deeptech as a new asset class for global allocators (Skyroot opens the floodgate), Canadian power-and-minerals plays as the unrecognised AI-infra trade, and DePIN/second-life GPU markets as the tail end of Goldman's $7.6T number that nobody is pricing yet. The convergence of all three is not a headline anywhere this weekend. That's the trade.

⚡ SIGNAL SPOTLIGHT
The "second-life GPU economy" will be the highest-IRR slice of Goldman's $7.6T AI build — and India and Canada are the two countries best-positioned to dominate it.
Goldman's report flags that frontier labs are rotating to new chip architectures every 12 months while accountants are depreciating the same chips over 4–6 years. NVIDIA's older A100s and H100s already rent at prices implying 5–6+ year useful lives — but only if a marketplace exists for inference workloads at scale. India has the inference demand (1.4B users + cheap labour for prompt-engineering layers) and Canada has the cheap hydroelectric power + cool climate that makes second-life GPU farms economic. The DePIN networks (Render, Akash, Bittensor) are the rails. The first sovereign or pension-backed second-life GPU fund — likely a CDPQ or CPP-backed Canadian play, or a NIIF-backed Indian play — will be the asymmetric trade of 2026 that nobody is pitching yet.
Time horizon: 6–18 months for first sovereign-anchored vehicle

★ THE LEAD
AI · GLOBAL CAPEX ▲ 2026–2031 (6 yr) ACT
Goldman puts $7.6 trillion on the AI buildout — and the chip-depreciation gap is the asymmetric trade nobody is pricing

Goldman Sachs Global Research dropped "Tracking Trillions" this week, modeling cumulative global AI infrastructure capex of $7.6T from 2026–2031: $5.1T on chips, $2.15T on data centers, $358B on power. The 2026 number alone is $765B; by 2031 it's $1.6T per year. Hidden in the report is a sensitivity analysis that flips the conventional debate. Demand is the talked-about variable; supply assumptions are doing the heavy lifting. Shortening chip economic life from 5 years to 3 (which NVIDIA's annual architecture refresh is already forcing) adds $1T/year in depreciation costs alone.

SO WHAT — INDIA: India sits at the demand end of this chain. With 1.4B users and the fastest-growing inference market globally, India is the natural home for second-life GPUs running Hindi/regional-language inference. Watch for an NIIF or sovereign-anchored second-life GPU fund within 12 months. Paytm's "AI as operating system" comment this week is the early signal — Indian platform companies WILL absorb depreciated foreign-frontier compute at a fraction of training cost.
SO WHAT — 🍁 CANADA: Canada is the supply end. Cheap hydroelectric (Quebec, Manitoba, BC), cold climate (cooling cost advantage), regulated power markets, and a sovereign-grade pension complex (CDPQ, CPP, OTPP) that can write multi-billion checks. The Bruce Power 4,800 MW announcement last week is the unspoken pre-positioning. Carney's Ottawa has 90 days to recognise this is the answer to both the −111K FT YTD jobs print AND the trade dependency on the US.
ACTION: Build a thesis note this week tracking (1) any sovereign or pension co-investment with hyperscaler / DePIN networks, (2) Indian deeptech follow-on rounds post-Skyroot (asset class signaling), (3) Carney federal cabinet shuffle or industrial-policy speech naming AI infrastructure. Position size accordingly across both portfolios.
💭 Bear case: Goldman's $7.6T is the peak-bubble number. NVIDIA's 75% data-center GPU gross margins have to compress eventually; if compute prices fall faster than utilization scales, the second-life GPU market never opens because new chips become cheap enough to supplant rather than augment. India's inference economics also depend on power tariffs that are politically unstable. Canada's hydro advantage is real but transmission to data center hubs is a 10-year build-out, not a 2-year one. The asymmetric trade may be 2030, not 2026.

🌱 THE LONG GAME

The 2025–2026 era of co-locating AI compute with hyperscaler urban data centers is ending. The 2027–2031 era — driven by Goldman's $358B power bill — is geographic dispersion: where power is, where cooling is free, where land is cheap. The implication for India and Canada is not a tactical trade. It's a generational asset-class shift. The countries that adapt their infrastructure-policy frameworks to host distributed AI compute (rather than concentrated hyperscaler campuses) win the second half of Goldman's curve. Both India and Canada have the natural endowments. Neither has the policy framework yet. The first one to announce one captures a decade of compounding flows.
