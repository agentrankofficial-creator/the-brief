Editor's Read
Suhail Seth · June 3, 2026

The single most important thing that happened today is small enough to miss: OpenAI shipped six role-specific Codex plug-ins — investment banking, public-equity investing, sales, the lot — and quietly wired FactSet, S&P, PitchBook, Moody's and Hebbia in behind them. That is not a developer-tool update. That is the moment the coding agent stopped pretending its market was 30 million engineers and admitted the real prize is a billion desk workers. Non-developers are already 20% of Codex's 5 million weekly users and growing three times faster than the coders.

Now connect three things nobody is putting in the same sentence. One: the capital wave funding all of this is no longer just American — Anthropic's $50B round ($965B mark) helped push May venture to $92B, Cerebras priced and popped, and today Bloomberg has DeepSeek closing $7.4B at $52–59B. Two: every market that ran today ran on the same fuel and the same bottleneck — power. India's BSE Power index is up 23% while the broad market is down 9%; the grid just hit 270 GW. Canada's TSX printed a record 35,170 on energy, and Ottawa is standing up a Tech Growth Fund plus a $25B Canada Strong Fund partly to build 100-MW AI data centres. Three: the currencies financing the import side of all this — the rupee at 95, the loonie at 1.39 — are both being quietly squeezed by the same strong-dollar, high-oil vice.

So here is my call: capability is being financialized three ways at once — as a product (vertical agents), as an asset (the IPO/round supercycle), and as sovereign infrastructure (Ottawa's fund, India's grid build). The thing to watch is not which lab wins. It's which incumbent data-and-distribution toll-collector — a FactSet, a Salesforce — gets disintermediated first when the agent owns the workflow. That is where the next great margin transfer happens.

The Lead
AI / ENTERPRISE ↑ 2–4 quarters ACT
OpenAI takes Codex off the developer's desk and sits it down at the banker's — six role-built agents, and the data terminals are now just plumbing

On June 2 OpenAI shipped six role-specific Codex plug-ins — investment banking, public-equity investing, sales, data analytics, creative production and product design — plus "Sites" (agents that build hosted apps) and annotations across documents, sheets and slides. The IB plug-in syncs institutional feeds including Moody's, FactSet, LSEG, S&P, PitchBook, Datasite and Hebbia to turn diligence into pitch books; the sales plug-in wires in Salesforce, HubSpot, Outreach and Clay. Codex now has 5M+ weekly active users, up 6x since February, and non-developers are ~20% of them — growing three times faster than developers. Microsoft (with its "Scout" enterprise agent) and Snowflake pushed the same enterprise-agent message the same week.

SO WHAT: This is the inflection where "AI coding assistant" becomes "AI white-collar labor." For any operator running services businesses, the addressable cost line shifts from the 5–10% of staff who write code to the 50–70% who push spreadsheets, decks and CRM entries. The margin question stops being "can we automate engineering" and becomes "which back-office desk function survives as a headcount line at all."

ACTION: Pilot one vertical agent against one real workflow (deck production, account reviews, financial modeling) this quarter and measure the payroll-to-AI-cost ratio. The operators who instrument that ratio now will be repricing labour 3–4 quarters before the ones who wait for a case study.
💭 Here's the version where this is wrong: enterprise procurement, data-governance and liability kill turnkey agents in regulated functions. Banks won't let an OpenAI agent touch a live pitch with client data; FactSet and Bloomberg re-paper their licences to choke the feeds; and "20% non-developers" is mostly curiosity, not production seats. In that world this is a demo, not a disruption, and the terminals keep their pricing power for years.

The strategic tell is the data-feed list. By making Moody's/FactSet/S&P/PitchBook into inputs to the agent rather than destinations the analyst visits, OpenAI moves the interface up one layer — and historically the layer that owns the interface eventually owns the economics (see how the browser commoditized the OS, how the app store taxed the developer). OpenAI also disclosed $4B+ in funding tied to enterprise integration and an open plug-in ecosystem (Wix, Replit, Figma, Webflow as Sites partners). The forthcoming verticals — corporate finance, legal, private equity, strategy consulting — are precisely the high-margin professional-services categories. Watch for the first named bulge-bracket or Big-Four production deployment; that's the signal this crossed from pilots to procurement.

Signal Board
VENTURE / IPO↑monthsWATCH
The exit window cracked open and capital is flooding back in — May venture hit $92B, the second-biggest month ever, and over half of it was one company

Crunchbase pegs global venture funding at $92B in May — the second-highest monthly total on record — with more than half ($50B) flowing into Anthropic's round at a $965B valuation. Cerebras priced its IPO at $185 (above range), raised $5.55B, and popped to a ~$86B fully-diluted valuation on debut, the first big pure-play AI listing in months. Defense-tech startups have already raised $14.6B this year, smashing 2025's full-year $9.6B record (Anduril, Shield AI, Saronic). SpaceX and others are queued.

SO WHAT: A reopening IPO market plus record private rounds is a self-reinforcing loop — exits return cash to LPs, who re-commit to funds, who write the next mega-round. For any operator weighing a capital raise or a sale, the window for AI-adjacent and infrastructure assets is as open as it has been in three years. The risk is that the same loop is concentrating a historic share of all capital into a handful of names.

ACTION: If you're holding an asset with a credible AI or compute-infrastructure narrative, this is a tape that rewards moving, not waiting. Pressure-test the concentration risk before assuming the window stays open past Q3.
💭 The bear version: $92B "venture funding" where 54% is a single mega-round isn't a healthy market — it's one whale and a lot of plankton. Cerebras already gave back a chunk of its pop. If Anthropic's eventual IPO prices below its $965B private mark, the whole liquidity-recycling thesis stalls and 2026 looks like 2021's hangover, not its boom.

AI / CHINA↑weeksWATCH
DeepSeek is closing $7.4B at up to $59B — and the cap table is a who's-who of the Chinese state-adjacent economy, not Silicon Valley

Bloomberg reports DeepSeek is close to sealing ~50 billion yuan ($7.4B) in its first external round — one of China's largest-ever startup financings — at a $52–59B valuation, up from a ~$20B whisper just weeks ago. Backers include Tencent, battery giant CATL, NetEase and JD.com, with founder Liang Wenfeng committing $2.9B of his own money. Axios flagged the same "DeepSeek enters the cash chat" story today.

SO WHAT: The contrast is the whole point — Anthropic at $965B, DeepSeek at ~$55B, for arguably comparable model capability. Either US AI is wildly overvalued, or Chinese AI is being deliberately financed on a capital-efficiency doctrine the West can't match. For anyone modelling the cost curve of AI inputs, DeepSeek is the data point arguing the floor on capability pricing is far lower than the IPO supercycle implies.

WATCH: Whether the round actually closes at the top of the $52–59B band, and whether any non-Chinese capital is in it. The composition tells you whether this is a market financing or an industrial-policy one.
💭 The other read: DeepSeek's "efficiency" is partly subsidized compute and opaque accounting, and a $55B mark on a company that can't freely buy frontier GPUs is its own kind of fiction. The valuation gap may reflect real constraints — export controls, capital mobility — not Western excess.

INDIA / MARKETS↑structuralACT
India's power stocks are the one green corner of a red market — BSE Power up 23% while the broad index falls 9%, and the heat is doing the buying

The Ken's Seetharaman lays it out: since January the BSE Power index (18 producers, grid operators and equipment makers) is up 23% while the BSE 100 is down 9% — a 32-point spread — and the index hit an all-time high last week. The driver is brutally physical: peak electricity demand crossed 270 GW in late May, weeks ahead of forecast, as a nationwide heatwave forced the power ministry to appeal for conservation. Over five years BSE Power has tripled the broad market's return.

SO WHAT: This is the cleanest expression of the year's real macro trade: in a market wary of FPI outflows, a weak rupee and an Iran-risk premium, the one theme that compounds regardless is electricity demand. AI data centres, EVs, manufacturing reshoring and air-conditioning all pull the same lever. Power is becoming the asset that's long every other secular trend at once — and the market has noticed.

ACTION: Treat grid, generation and power-equipment exposure as the structural India position, not a tactical one. The catalyst isn't a quarter — it's a decade of demand growth meeting a supply system already appealing for restraint at 270 GW.
💭 The version where this breaks: a 23%-vs-(-9%) spread is exactly what a crowded, late-cycle momentum trade looks like right before it mean-reverts. Power stocks at all-time highs on a heatwave are pricing perpetual scarcity; one mild monsoon, a tariff reset, or a capex glut and the "structural" trade gives back two years in two months.

INDIA / MACRO↑this weekWATCH
India's factories are quietly accelerating into the RBI's rate week — capital goods just clocked a fourth straight month of double digits, but the consumer is missing

Per Finshots, April IIP rose 4.9% (up from 3.2% in March), with manufacturing at 6.2% — comfortably above the FY26 average — and capital goods/infrastructure output growing 9–11% YoY, a fourth consecutive double-digit month. The catch: consumer non-durables (everyday FMCG) grew a feeble 0.7%, signalling households are still cautious. MoSPI also rebased the index to 2022–23 and will now revise sector weights annually. The RBI sets rates this week.

SO WHAT: A capex-led, consumer-light recovery is a specific signal: investment and supply chains are absorbing the West-Asia oil shock without cutting output, but the demand side hasn't broadened. For the RBI that argues against a panic hike — production isn't overheating — while the weak consumer argues against premature tightening. The base case is a hold with a close eye on the oil-driven inflation pulse.

WATCH: The RBI decision this week, and specifically the language on inflation vs. growth. A capex-strong / consumer-weak split is the exact configuration where the central bank's tone matters more than the rate itself.
💭 Counter-case: a 0.7% consumer-goods print isn't "caution," it's the leading edge of demand destruction from a year of elevated fuel and food prices. If capex is the only cylinder firing, the recovery is narrower and more fragile than the 4.9% headline flatters — and one bad inflation print forces the RBI's hand.

CHINA / POLICY→quartersNOTE
Xi puts "future industries" at the center of the June Qiushi — while Beijing's own chip export-control machinery is described as dysfunctional

Sinocism (Bill Bishop) flags the June 1 Qiushi leading with Xi's speech from the January 30 Politburo study session on developing "future industries," alongside new outbound-investment regulations and reporting on chip export-control dysfunction. The throughline: Beijing is simultaneously trying to direct capital into frontier sectors at home and tighten the rules on how that capital and technology leave the country — even as its own controls misfire.

SO WHAT: This is the policy substrate beneath the DeepSeek round. When top leadership codifies "future industries" as doctrine and walls off outbound capital, state-adjacent financings like DeepSeek's stop looking like venture deals and start looking like industrial policy executed through private balance sheets. For anyone tracking the US–China AI bifurcation, the signal is that China's AI capital base is being deliberately ring-fenced and domestically steered.

WATCH: Whether the outbound-investment rules tighten further this quarter — that's the variable that determines how isolated the two AI capital markets become.
💭 The skeptical read: Qiushi lead articles are ritual, not roadmap — "future industries" has been a slogan for years with patchy follow-through, and "export-control dysfunction" cuts against the idea of a coordinated grand strategy. This may be aspiration dressed as direction.

🍁 MARKETS↑nowNOTE
The TSX clears 35,000 for the first time ever — and like everywhere else, it was energy that carried it over the line

Canada's main index closed at a record 35,170 on June 2 (+1.2%), its first finish above 35,000. Energy led, up ~2.4% on the day with Canadian Natural +2.8%, Imperial Oil +3% and Cenovus +4% as oil held near $95; contract manufacturer Celestica jumped ~11%. US indices set records the same session.

SO WHAT: The same trade powering India's BSE Power index and the US chip rally is lifting Bay Street — high oil plus an AI-infrastructure bid. For a Canadian operator the record is less a "risk-on" all-clear than confirmation that the index is now a leveraged bet on energy prices and a thin band of tech (Celestica, Shopify) rather than broad domestic strength — note it's hitting highs the same week the economy printed a technical recession.

💭 Where this is hollow: a record built on oil and one contract-manufacturer's 11% day is a narrow record. Strip energy and the AI-supply-chain names and the TSX looks like the recessionary economy underneath it.

🍁 POLICY / AI↑this weekACT
Ottawa is about to start taking equity in AI startups — a Tech Growth Fund, the $25B Canada Strong Fund, and a bid to stop founders fleeing south

Ahead of a national AI strategy expected this week, the Carney government will launch a Canadian Tech Growth Fund taking direct equity stakes in domestic AI startups (smaller cheques), while the $25B Canada Strong Fund sovereign vehicle backs larger "national champions." The plan tops up a $300M AI Compute Access Fund, funds the national AI institutes, makes Ottawa a "strategic anchor customer," and supports 100-MW AI data centres. The problem it targets is stark: under a third of high-potential Canadian-founded startups in 2024 were HQ'd in Canada (down from 67% in 2015–19), and US investors were in 80% of Canadian VC deals last year.

SO WHAT: Direct government equity in startups is an industrial-policy turn — the same instinct visible in Beijing's "future industries" push and Washington's chip-equity stakes. For founders and operators in Canada it changes the funding calculus: there's about to be patient domestic capital that doesn't demand the company relocate to access it. The open question is whether the cheque sizes are large enough to out-compete US risk appetite, not just match it.

ACT: If you touch the Canadian innovation ecosystem, read the full strategy the moment it drops and map eligibility — anchor-customer status and Compute Access Fund subsidies are concrete, near-term levers, not just headline capital.
💭 The failure case: Canada has announced versions of this before and the structural gaps — risk-averse pensions that skip rounds as "too small," shallow late-stage capital, better US exits — don't yield to a fund. Government equity can even crowd out or signal-pollute. Talent stays world-class; the capital and ambition still migrate.

🍁 TRADE→2026–27WATCH
Canada formally asks for a 16-year CUSMA renewal — LeBlanc moves first, knowing the Trump administration may not play along

Canada-US trade minister Dominic LeBlanc officially requested renewal of the North American free-trade pact for a full 16-year term, the longest extension available — a pre-emptive move to lock in certainty ahead of the formal CUSMA review, even as the Trump administration's posture remains unsettled.

SO WHAT: A 16-year ask is a bid for the one thing cross-border operators value most — predictability. For any business with supply chains or revenue spanning the border, the length of the renewal Ottawa secures (or fails to) is the single biggest variable for multi-year capital planning. The goods-sector job losses already hitting Canada trace straight back to this uncertainty.

💭 The cynical read: asking for 16 years is theatre when the counterparty treats trade deals as renegotiable at will. The headline length means little if Washington reopens terms sector-by-sector regardless.

The India trade hiding in plain sight: own the bottleneck, not the application — power and grid sit upstream of every AI, EV and manufacturing rupee

With BSE Power +23% YTD, peak demand at 270 GW, and capital-goods output in a fourth double-digit month, the upstream-of-everything theme is electricity and the equipment that moves it. Application-layer AI bets in India are crowded and richly priced; the picks-and-shovels layer (generation, transmission, grid equipment, cooling) is long every downstream trend simultaneously.

SO WHAT: The asymmetry favours the input layer — you don't have to pick the winning AI app if you own the power it runs on.

The Long Game
Power is quietly becoming the universal denominator of the AI age, and today three continents said the same thing in three accents. India's grid hit 270 GW and made power stocks the only winners in a falling market. Canada's record TSX was carried by energy, and its new AI strategy budgets for 100-MW data centres. The US chip rally and the Anthropic/Cerebras/DeepSeek capital wave are, underneath the silicon, a bet on electricity that doesn't exist yet. The scarce input of the early 2020s was compute; the scarce input of the 2030s is the wattage to run it. Whoever controls generation, transmission and cooling — sovereign or private — ends up holding a call option on every other secular trend at once. That is the trade to be early and patient on, across every geography Suhail operates in.
