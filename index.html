EDITOR'S READ
Suhail Seth · May 29, 2026

Markets spent today chasing a disinflation mirage. The US 10-year fell to 4.47% (off the 4.7% high from mid-May), the Canadian 10-year to 3.45%, WTI back to roughly $90, Bitcoin under $77,000 — most of it on a rumour that turned out to be a forgery. Iranian state TV "released" a Strait of Hormuz memorandum the White House flatly called "a complete fabrication."

Strip out the head-fake and two hard facts survive the day. April PCE printed 3.8% headline and 3.3% core — the hottest in nearly three years. And $166B of tariffs the Supreme Court ruled unconstitutional now has to flow back to more than 300,000 importers, while a separate Section 122 tariff hits its 150-day statutory wall around July 24. Connect those: the bond market wants to price cuts off cheaper energy, but the inflation floor is structural and the fiscal plumbing is in open chaos.

The cross-border read is the same trade wearing two currencies. The rupee sits at a record-soft 96 as a hot dollar squeezes Indian importer margins; the loonie firmed to 1.38 and Canadian yields sank as the market front-runs a Bank of Canada cut on June 10 that this inflation data does not justify. Both currencies are leaning on the same Fed-and-oil story.

My call: the "rate-cut summer" trade is premature. The refund is phantom stimulus, the ceasefire is unsigned, and PCE was the only number that didn't lie today. The question nobody is pricing — does Section 122's July 24 expiry become the real catalyst of the summer?

The $166B tariff refund is phantom stimulus — it will not reach the real economy, and the market is watching the wrong number.
Customs is paying in phases: of roughly $85B in refund claims accepted, only about $20.6B has actually been returned. Worse, consumer class-action firms are already suing the brands that kept the refunds after passing the tariff cost through to shoppers — an "unjust windfall" fight that traps the cash between importers and consumers in litigation for the rest of 2026. The liquidity injection everyone expects gets strangled in court. The catalyst that actually matters is the one no one is pricing: Section 122's hard 150-day expiry around July 24, a tariff cliff that flips overnight from cost to vacuum.
Time horizon: 2–3 months (refund drip through 2026; Section 122 cliff late July)

The Lead
MACRO · TRADE→Now → late JulyWATCH
Washington must hand back $166B in unconstitutional tariffs — and a bigger tariff cliff lands July 24

The Supreme Court's February ruling in Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump held that the IEEPA never authorised the nationwide tariffs — leaving an estimated $166B owed back to more than 300,000 importers. The administration's backup move, a 10% global tariff under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974, was itself ruled unlawful by the Court of International Trade in a 2-1 decision on May 7; the Federal Circuit granted a temporary administrative stay around May 13 while the appeal runs. Crucially, Section 122 carries a hard 150-day statutory ceiling — it expires near July 24 unless Congress acts, which it shows no sign of doing.

SO WHAT: This is the single biggest unwind of executive trade power in a generation, and it cuts two ways for any cross-border operator. The refund is a balance-sheet event for importers, not a market stimulus — it drips out slowly and is already contested. The July 24 Section 122 expiry is the live wire: a multinational supply chain priced around a 10% universal tariff has to re-plan landed-cost and sourcing on a sub-90-day clock, in both directions. Exporters into the US — Indian and Canadian alike — get a potential tailwind; importers who built tariff pass-through into pricing face a margin reset.
ACTION: Map any US-facing import/export exposure to the July 24 date now, not in July. Treat the refund as a contingent receivable trapped in litigation, not cash. Watch the Federal Circuit docket for whether the administrative stay becomes permanent.
💭 Here's the version where this is wrong: Congress quietly extends Section 122 or the White House finds a third statutory hook before July 24, the Federal Circuit makes the stay permanent on appeal, and the refund machinery keeps the $166B in a slow enough drip that nothing actually reprices. Tariff policy has already survived two court losses — assuming the third kills it may be the rookie error.
Read original → Milk Road Macro
Refund mechanics: Customs launched the CAPE portal on April 20; about $85B in claims have been accepted and roughly $20.6B actually returned, prioritising recent payments first — so capital trickles into corporate reserves across the rest of 2026. The secondary crisis is legal: consumer class-action firms are suing brands and retailers that raised prices to offset tariffs, arguing that keeping the refund is an "unjust windfall." Section 122 was implemented under the Trade Act's balance-of-payments provision; the CIT found contemporary conditions don't present the "large and serious" deficits the statute requires. The government keeps collecting from non-plaintiff importers during the appeal — so the liability grows even as the legal ground shrinks.

Signal Board
GEOPOLITICS · ENERGY→DaysACT
A forged Hormuz "peace deal" moved oil and Bitcoin before the White House called it "a complete fabrication"

Iranian state TV "released" a memorandum claiming maritime traffic through the Strait of Hormuz would normalise within a month of an interim deal — while Iran kept the right to inspect vessels and charge wartime service fees. The White House said the report was "not true" and the MoU "a complete fabrication." Yet underneath the forgery, US and Iranian negotiators do say they reached a genuine draft to extend the ceasefire and gradually reopen Hormuz — pending final sign-off from Trump and Iran's Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei. Oil erased the day's gains on the ceasefire-extension report; Bitcoin slid under $77,000 after fresh US strikes near Bandar Abbas earlier in the session.

SO WHAT: The new development isn't "peace" — it's that the market now trades headlines it can't authenticate. A fabricated MoU was enough to pull WTI off its highs and drag yields down with it. For any operator with energy-linked input costs or petro-sensitive FX, the lesson is that the Hormuz risk premium is now a two-way coin-flip on unverified Tehran-sourced claims. India (a major Gulf-crude importer) and Canada (a net oil exporter) sit on opposite ends of every one of these whipsaws.
ACTION: Don't chase the de-escalation rally on unsigned drafts. Treat any "Hormuz reopens" print as unconfirmed until Trump and Khamenei both sign. Energy hedges stay on.
💭 The bull case: the forgery is a trial balloon, the real draft is closer than anyone admits, and within weeks Hormuz reopens, oil falls to the $70s, and the disinflation trade that looked premature today turns out to have been early-right.

MACRO · INFLATION↑QuartersWATCH
April PCE ran 3.8% — the hottest in nearly three years — and the rate-cut crowd looked the other way

The Fed's preferred gauge came in at 3.8% headline year-on-year with core at 3.3% (a two-year-plus high) and a 0.4% monthly print. On any normal day that's a hawkish shock. Instead, the 10-year Treasury fell to 4.47% as cheaper energy on the Hormuz ceasefire chatter dominated the tape. The bond market chose the energy story over the inflation story.

SO WHAT: This is the cleanest "inflation isn't dead" data point of the month, and it sits directly under every rate-cut bet now embedded in North American curves. If energy relief proves to be a head-fake, the disinflation that justified today's yield drop reverses fast. The structural-inflation regime — sticky services, fiscal disorder, a tariff system in legal limbo — argues the floor is higher than the curve assumes.
ACTION: Fade duration-long positioning that's leaning entirely on energy. The next CPI/PCE pair is the tell on whether 3.8% was a peak or a base.
💭 The dovish read: April is the high-water mark, the tariff unwind is itself disinflationary as the $166B and Section 122 costs come out of prices, and by autumn PCE is rolling toward 3% — in which case the bond market front-ran the turn correctly today.

OPPORTUNITY · GLOBAL↑To July 24ACT
The Section 122 expiry is a dated catalyst hiding in plain sight

A hard statutory cliff near July 24 on the 10% universal tariff is the rare macro event with a known date and an unknown market reaction. Importers re-rate up on relief; exporters who'd gained on US protectionism lose an edge; supply chains reprice landed cost on a clock.

SO WHAT: Dated catalysts are tradable in a way that open-ended geopolitics isn't. Indian and Canadian exporters into the US should model the post-July-24 cost curve now; US importers should treat refund receivables as optionality, not cash.
OPPORTUNITY · INDIA↓QuartersWATCH
A rupee pinned near 96 keeps the importer-margin squeeze on — and the integration premium intact

With the rupee at a record-soft 96 on a hot dollar, Indian businesses that import inputs and can't reprice keep bleeding margin, while vertically integrated and export-oriented names hold the advantage. The FX regime, not demand, is the swing factor.

SO WHAT: The durable read for an India operator: in a weak-rupee regime, pricing power and supply-chain integration are the moat. Watch RBI's tolerance for further depreciation versus intervention — the size and cadence of its FX operations is the signal of whether 96 is a line or a way-station.

The Long Game
The quiet structural story under this week's tariff chaos is the reassertion of Article I. Two courts have now told the executive it cannot levy nationwide tariffs by emergency decree, and the $166B refund is the price of having tried. Whatever happens to Section 122 on July 24, the precedent is being set that US trade policy — the single most market-moving lever of the past two years — runs through Congress and the courts, not the Oval Office alone. For cross-border operators who learned to trade every tariff headline, the slow-burn shift is that the next tariff regime will be slower, more litigated, and more predictable. That's less drama and more durability — and durability is tradable.
